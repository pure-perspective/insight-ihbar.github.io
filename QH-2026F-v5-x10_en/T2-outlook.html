PHYS598500 · T2 Outlook · Week 2–4

From Artificial Atoms to Protected Qubits

Sequence, Interfaces, and Advanced-Learning Map for the Fifteen Questions

What Are These Three Weeks Answering Together?

Scope: W2: circuit quantization and Josephson anharmonicity; W3: tunability and the C-shunted Transmon; W4: noise, Fluxonium, and Hamiltonian engineering

Shared question: How do we first use a circuit to create a controllable quantum spectrum, and then redesign the Hamiltonian backward from the error operator we want to suppress?

Direction of the fifteen questions: artificial atoms → circuit quantization → computational subspace → Josephson anharmonicity → tunability → C-shunt → Transmon trade-off → noise operator → protection → Fluxonium → Hamiltonian engineering

The three weeks form one continuous argument. W2 establishes how a Josephson circuit becomes an addressable artificial atom. W3 asks how SQUID tunability and a shunt capacitor reshape that atom into a practical Transmon, while exposing control parameters as possible noise channels. W4 then uses the same language in reverse: once we know which operator the environment uses to create errors, can we design eigenstates, symmetries, and Hamiltonian terms that make those errors less likely?

T2 Overview of the Fifteen Questions

Week 2: First Establish That a Circuit Can Become an Atom

Question 1 → Question 2: From Existence to Circuit Variables

Question 1 first explains why a macroscopic circuit can still behave quantum mechanically, so students do not initially mistake circuit quantization for a purely formal exercise. But saying only that “superconductors are quantum” is not enough to write a Hamiltonian, so Question 2 first introduces the more concrete flux-charge pair \((\Phi,Q)\).

Question 2 → Question 3: From Circuit Variables to a Quantum Hamiltonian

Once flux and charge are identified, Question 3 translates capacitors and inductors into energy terms and promotes \((\Phi,Q)\) to quantum operators. This keeps the construction of the Hamiltonian separate from the harder phase-number representation introduced later.

Question 3 → Question 4: A Discrete Spectrum Is Not Yet a Qubit

Quantizing an LC mode produces discrete but equally spaced levels. Question 4 therefore separates the existence of eigenstates from the ability to isolate and selectively address a computational subspace.

Question 4 → Question 5: The Failure Condition Determines the Required Element

The harmonic oscillator fails because all adjacent transitions have the same frequency. Question 5 introduces the harder phase-number pair \((\varphi,n)\) only when it becomes physically necessary, then follows the Josephson cosine potential to an anharmonic spectrum, leakage suppression, and frequency-selective control.

W2 progression: Existence of a quantum mode → identify \((\Phi,Q)\) → construct and quantize the Hamiltonian → define the computational subspace → introduce \((\varphi,n)\) and Josephson anharmonicity.

Week 3: From Tunability to the C-Shunted Transmon

Question 5 → Question 6: From an Addressable Qubit to a Useful Operating Frequency

Josephson anharmonicity makes selective control possible, but a processor also needs frequency allocation, calibration, and controllable interactions. Question 6 motivates tunability before introducing the circuit mechanism that realizes it.

Question 6 → Question 7: Tunability Requires a Flux-Dependent Josephson Energy

Question 7 introduces the SQUID as a circuit element whose effective Josephson energy depends on external flux. Students can then connect a control knob to a Hamiltonian parameter and to the qubit transition frequency.

Question 7 → Question 8: Tunability Alone Does Not Produce a Robust Qubit

After establishing flux tunability, Question 8 follows one concrete circuit modification—the shunt capacitor—through \(C\uparrow\), \(E_C\downarrow\), reduced charge dispersion, and the emergence of the Transmon regime.

Question 8 → Question 9: A Parameter Regime Is a Design Trade-off

Increasing \(E_J/E_C\) suppresses charge dispersion but also reduces anharmonicity. Question 9 makes students balance charge sensitivity, leakage, and gate control instead of treating “larger \(E_J/E_C\)” as an unlimited improvement.

Question 9 → Question 10: The Same Knob Can Carry Control and Noise

W3 ends by observing that a parameter used for control can also fluctuate. Question 10 introduces \(\delta H=(\partial H/\partial\lambda)\delta\lambda(t)\) and the corresponding transition matrix element, providing the conceptual bridge to a quantitative treatment of noise in W4.

W3 progression: Motivate tunability → implement it with a SQUID → add a C-shunt → balance the Transmon regime → recognize control parameters as noise channels.

Week 4: Shift from Noise Transitions to Protected-Qubit Design

Question 10 → Question 11: From a Noise Channel to a Transition Rate

Question 10 identifies how a fluctuating parameter enters the Hamiltonian. Question 11 adds the missing environmental information and combines the operator matrix element with the noise spectrum at the transition frequency.

\[ \Gamma_{i\to f}\propto |\langle f|\hat O|i\rangle|^2S_F(\omega_{fi}). \]

Question 11 → Question 12: Define Protection without Losing Control

Question 12 distinguishes sweet spots, symmetry-based selection rules, and small-overlap suppression. The control operator remains explicit so that suppressing an environmental transition is not confused with making the qubit uncontrollable.

Question 12 → Question 13: Fluxonium as the First Concrete Realization

Only after the protection criteria are established does Question 13 introduce the superinductor and the Hamiltonian term \(\tfrac12E_L(\varphi-\varphi_{\rm ext})^2\).

Question 13 → Question 14: Follow the New Term into the Eigenstates

Question 14 traces how \(E_C,E_J,E_L,\Phi_{\rm ext}\) shape the potential, localization, parity, transition frequencies, and matrix elements while keeping dephasing and relaxation distinct.

Question 14 → Question 15: Generalize Fluxonium into Hamiltonian Engineering

Question 15 turns the Fluxonium example into a reusable design method: begin with the dominant noise operator, impose protection and control criteria, and then compare \(0-\pi\), \(\cos2\varphi\), and the ferro-transmon.

W4 progression: Quantify noise-induced transitions → define protection with retained control → add a superinductor → shape wavefunctions and matrix elements → design backward from the target error.

Shared Formula Framework for the Fifteen Questions

1. LC oscillator: discrete but equally spaced

\[ \hat H_{LC}=\frac{\hat Q^2}{2C}+\frac{\hat\Phi^2}{2L} =\hbar\omega\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12\right) \]

2. Transmon: reduce charge dispersion by choosing a parameter regime

\[ \hat H_{\rm T}=4E_C(\hat n-n_g)^2-E_J\cos\hat\varphi \]

3. Transitions: both operator matrix elements and the environmental spectrum matter

\[ \hat V(t)=\hat O F(t),\qquad \Gamma_{i\to f}\propto|\langle f|\hat O|i\rangle|^2S_F(\omega_{fi}) \]

4. Fluxonium: add inductive energy and external-flux control

\[ \hat H_{\rm F}=4E_C\hat n^2-E_J\cos\hat\varphi +\frac12E_L(\hat\varphi-\varphi_{\rm ext})^2 \]

5. Four protected-qubit criteria

\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial\omega_{01}}{\partial\lambda}\approx0,\\ &\langle0|\hat O_{\rm noise}|1\rangle\approx0,\\ &\langle0|\hat O_{\rm drive}|1\rangle\neq0,\\ &\Delta_{\rm leakage}\ \text{sufficiently large}. \end{aligned} \]

Two Deliberately Retained Cross-Week Tasks

W2: Infer the Hamiltonian Backward from a Chip Image

The geometry → parameter → Hamiltonian → spectrum/control chain in Version 5 becomes a reverse-design assignment. Before they have full architecture knowledge, students need only propose one evidence-bounded hypothesis for a Transmon cell and identify which missing information requires simulation, fabrication data, or measurement.

W4: When Should Fluxonium Be Chosen over a Transmon?

This question is worth retaining, but W4 should not pretend to complete it. Students first leave a provisional answer; after adding control, coupling, readout, thermalization, fabrication, and yield in sequence, they revisit the architecture decision in W16. The difference between the two answers is evidence of cross-layer learning.

Toward W5: From Eigenstate Structure to Control

By the end of T2, students know that the effectiveness of a drive depends on \(\langle0|\hat O_{\rm drive}|1\rangle\), and that anharmonicity and the leakage gap constrain selective control. W5 can therefore proceed naturally to time-dependent drives, the rotating frame, Rabi rates, and DRAG without turning pulse control into a list of techniques disconnected from the device Hamiltonian.
