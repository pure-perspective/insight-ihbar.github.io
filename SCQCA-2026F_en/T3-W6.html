Why a two-qubit gate

Gate set \(\{\mathrm{CNOT},H,T\}\), with \(T=\mathrm{diag}(1,e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}})\). For each \(n\)-qubit unitary \(U\) and each \(\varepsilon>0\), can a finite circuit \(V\) of these gates give

\[\min_{\phi}\left\|U-e^{i\phi}V\right\|<\varepsilon\ ?\]

Yes. This property is universality. The phase \(e^{i\phi}\) is a global phase and has no physical effect. Any entangling two-qubit gate can replace CNOT: the hardware uses CZ or iSWAP.

M. A. Nielsen and I. L. Chuang, Quantum Computation and Quantum Information (Cambridge, 2010), Sec. 4.5.

√iSWAP makes entanglement

\[\sqrt{\mathrm{iSWAP}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\0&\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}&\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}&0\\0&\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}&\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}&0\\0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\big(\sqrt{\mathrm{iSWAP}}\big)^2=\mathrm{iSWAP}\]

Start in \(|10\rangle\):

\[\sqrt{\mathrm{iSWAP}}\,|10\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|10\rangle+i|01\rangle\big)\]

Product test: \(c_{00}c_{11}=0\), but \(c_{01}c_{10}=\tfrac{i}{2}\). The test fails, thus the state is entangled.

The full iSWAP changes \(|10\rangle\) to \(i|01\rangle\). This is a product state.

Compiling to CZ

A Hadamard gate on the target changes \(Z\) into \(X\), because \(HZH=X\):

\[(\mathbb 1\otimes H)\,\mathrm{CZ}\,(\mathbb 1\otimes H)=|0\rangle\langle0|\otimes\mathbb 1+|1\rangle\langle1|\otimes X=\mathrm{CNOT}\]

CZ is symmetric. It has no control qubit and no target qubit.

A SWAP is three CNOTs. The middle CNOT has its control on qubit 2: \(b\to a\oplus b\), then \(a\to b\), then \(a\oplus b\to a\).

On a CZ machine, a SWAP costs three CZ gates.

Compiling to iSWAP

A CNOT costs two iSWAPs and three single-qubit gates. \(S^\dagger\) is a virtual Z gate.

\[\mathrm{iSWAP}=(S\otimes S)\,\mathrm{CZ}\,\mathrm{SWAP},\qquad S=\mathrm{diag}(1,i)\]

An iSWAP is a SWAP with a CZ and two S gates. Thus \(\mathrm{SWAP}=(S^\dagger\otimes S^\dagger)\,\mathrm{CZ}\,\mathrm{iSWAP}\).

All circuits are correct up to a global phase.

N. Schuch and J. Siewert, Phys. Rev. A 67, 032301 (2003).

CCNOT from CNOTs and T gates

On a CZ machine, each CNOT is one CZ and two H gates: six CZ gates. The T gates are virtual Z gates.

With \(c=1\), the output on \(c\) is \(\mathrm{NAND}(a,b)\). Thus a processor with these gates can also run all classical logic.

M. A. Nielsen and I. L. Chuang, Quantum Computation and Quantum Information (Cambridge, 2010), Fig. 4.9.

Fixed or tunable: four designs

Fixed qubits, fixed \(g\)

Control: Cross-resonance, a drive on Q1 at \(\omega_2\).

+ Fewest lines. No flux noise.

− Static ZZ. Frequency crowding. Slow gates.

Tunable qubits, fixed \(g\)

Control: Flux moves Q1 to a resonance (iSWAP, CZ).

+ Fast gates. Flux avoids collisions.

− Static ZZ. Flux noise.

Fixed qubits, coupler

Control: Flux on the coupler turns ZZ on (CZ).

+ \(g_{\rm eff}\approx0\) at idle. No flux noise on the qubits.

− Frequency crowding.

Tunable qubits, coupler

Control: Flux on the qubits and the coupler (CZ, iSWAP).

+ \(g_{\rm eff}\approx0\) at idle. Fast gates.

− Most lines. Flux noise.

A coupling capacitor: the Lagrangian

The voltage on each capacitor, with ground at 0, is:

\[V_{C_1}=V_1,\qquad V_{C_2}=V_2,\qquad V_{C_g}=V_1-V_2\]

The junction phases obey \(\dot\varphi_j=\frac{2eV_j}{\hbar}\). The junction energy is \(U_J=-E_{J1}\cos\varphi_1-E_{J2}\cos\varphi_2\).

\[T=\frac12C_1V_1^2+\frac12C_2V_2^2+\frac12C_g\left(V_1-V_2\right)^2\]

The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal L=T-U_J\). Expand the \(C_g\) term:

\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal L&=\frac12\left(C_1+C_g\right)V_1^2+\frac12\left(C_2+C_g\right)V_2^2-C_gV_1V_2\\&\quad{}+E_{J1}\cos\varphi_1+E_{J2}\cos\varphi_2\end{aligned}\]

Substitute \(V_j=\frac{\hbar}{2e}\,\dot\varphi_j\):

\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal L(\varphi,\dot\varphi)&=\left(\frac{\hbar}{2e}\right)^2\Big[\frac12\left(C_1+C_g\right)\dot\varphi_1^2+\frac12\left(C_2+C_g\right)\dot\varphi_2^2\\&\qquad{}-C_g\,\dot\varphi_1\dot\varphi_2\Big]+E_{J1}\cos\varphi_1+E_{J2}\cos\varphi_2\end{aligned}\]

A coupling capacitor: the Hamiltonian

The conjugate momenta, with \(p=\hbar n\), are:

\[p_1=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\varphi_1}=\frac{\hbar}{2e}\Big[\left(C_1+C_g\right)V_1-C_gV_2\Big]=\hbar n_1\]
\[p_2=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\varphi_2}=\frac{\hbar}{2e}\Big[\left(C_2+C_g\right)V_2-C_gV_1\Big]=\hbar n_2\]

Write them as a matrix. Then solve exactly for the voltages:

\[2e\begin{pmatrix}n_1\\n_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}C_1+C_g&-C_g\\-C_g&C_2+C_g\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}V_1\\V_2\end{pmatrix}\]
\[\begin{pmatrix}V_1\\V_2\end{pmatrix}=\frac{2e}{D}\begin{pmatrix}C_2+C_g&C_g\\C_g&C_1+C_g\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}n_1\\n_2\end{pmatrix}\]
\[D=C_1C_2+C_g\left(C_1+C_2\right)\]

The Hamiltonian is \(\mathcal H=p_1\dot\varphi_1+p_2\dot\varphi_2-\mathcal L=T+U_J\). Write the voltages with \(n_1\) and \(n_2\):

\[\mathcal H=4E_{C1}n_1^2-E_{J1}\cos\varphi_1+4E_{C2}n_2^2-E_{J2}\cos\varphi_2+\mathcal H_c\]
\[E_{C1}=\frac{e^2\left(C_2+C_g\right)}{2D},\qquad E_{C2}=\frac{e^2\left(C_1+C_g\right)}{2D}\]
\[\mathcal H_c=4e^2\,\frac{C_g}{D}\,n_1n_2\]

Use \(n=\tfrac{i}{2}\big(\tfrac{E_J}{2E_C}\big)^{\frac{1}{4}}(a^\dagger-a)\) and \(\hbar\omega_p=\sqrt{8E_JE_C}\):

\[\frac{\mathcal H_c}{\hbar}=-g\,(a_1^\dagger-a_1)(a_2^\dagger-a_2)\]
\[g=\frac{C_g\sqrt{\omega_{p1}\,\omega_{p2}}}{2\sqrt{\left(C_1+C_g\right)\left(C_2+C_g\right)}}\]

For \(C_g=0.32\) fF, \(C_1=C_2=80\) fF and \(\omega_p=2\pi\times5\) GHz, \(g\approx2\pi\times10\) MHz.

J. Koch et al., Phys. Rev. A 76, 042319 (2007).

Two transmons and one coupling

Each transmon is a ladder with its own \(\omega_1,\alpha_1\) and \(\omega_2,\alpha_2\):

\[\frac{\mathcal H}{\hbar}=\omega_1a_1^\dagger a_1+\frac{\alpha_1}{2}a_1^\dagger a_1^\dagger a_1a_1+\omega_2a_2^\dagger a_2+\frac{\alpha_2}{2}a_2^\dagger a_2^\dagger a_2a_2+\frac{\mathcal H_c}{\hbar}\]

The coupling capacitor gives:

\[\frac{\mathcal H_c}{\hbar}=-g\,(a_1^\dagger-a_1)(a_2^\dagger-a_2)\]
\[=g\,\big(a_1^\dagger a_2+a_1a_2^\dagger-a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger-a_1a_2\big)\]

Rotate each transmon at its own frequency:

\[U=\sum_{j,k}e^{-i(j\omega_1+k\omega_2)t}\,|jk\rangle\langle jk|,\qquad\mathcal H_{\rm rot}=U^\dagger\mathcal H U-i\hbar\,U^\dagger\dot U\]

Each operator gets its own phase:

\[U^\dagger a_jU=a_j\,e^{-i\omega_jt},\qquad U^\dagger a_j^\dagger U=a_j^\dagger\,e^{i\omega_jt}\]

The term \(-i\hbar\,U^\dagger\dot U\) cancels the \(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\) terms. The \(\alpha\) terms do not turn. Keep all terms:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\mathcal H_{\rm rot}}{\hbar}&=\frac{\alpha_1}{2}a_1^\dagger a_1^\dagger a_1a_1+\frac{\alpha_2}{2}a_2^\dagger a_2^\dagger a_2a_2+g\,\big(e^{i\Delta t}a_1^\dagger a_2+e^{-i\Delta t}a_1a_2^\dagger\big)\\&\quad{}-g\,\big(e^{i(\omega_1+\omega_2)t}a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger+e^{-i(\omega_1+\omega_2)t}a_1a_2\big)\end{aligned}\]

\(\Delta\equiv\omega_1-\omega_2\) is slow. \(\omega_1+\omega_2\approx2\pi\times10\) GHz is fast, and its average is zero (RWA):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\mathcal H_{\rm rot}}{\hbar}&\overset{\rm RWA}{\simeq}\frac{\alpha_1}{2}a_1^\dagger a_1^\dagger a_1a_1+\frac{\alpha_2}{2}a_2^\dagger a_2^\dagger a_2a_2\\&\quad{}+g\,\big(e^{i\Delta t}a_1^\dagger a_2+e^{-i\Delta t}a_1a_2^\dagger\big)\end{aligned}\]

\(|01\rangle\) meets \(|10\rangle\): iSWAP

On resonance, \(\Delta=0\), and the phase factors are equal to 1:

\[i\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}c_{01}\\c_{10}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&g\\g&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{01}\\c_{10}\end{pmatrix}\]

Start in \(|10\rangle\):

\[c_{10}(t)=\cos gt,\qquad c_{01}(t)=-i\sin gt\]

The excitation moves between the two qubits at the rate \(g\). Stop at:

\[gt=\frac{\pi}{2}:\quad|10\rangle\to-i|01\rangle,\quad|01\rangle\to-i|10\rangle\]

The result is an iSWAP gate with \(-i\) in place of \(i\). A Z gate on one qubit changes \(-i\) to \(i\). At \(gt=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\), the result is a \(\sqrt{\mathrm{iSWAP}}\) gate.

\[t=\frac{\pi}{2g}=25\text{ ns}\quad\text{for }g=2\pi\times10\text{ MHz}\]

With level 2, \(|11\rangle\) also gets a small phase. In Google's Sycamore gate, this phase is \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\).

F. Arute et al., Nature 574, 505 (2019).

A tunable coupler

When the coupler is far from both qubits, \(g_{1c}\ll|\omega_1-\omega_c|\) and \(g_{2c}\ll|\omega_2-\omega_c|\):

\[g_{\rm eff}\approx g_{12}+\frac{g_{1c}g_{2c}}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{\omega_1-\omega_c}+\frac{1}{\omega_2-\omega_c}\Big)\]

Two transmon qubits and one transmon coupler. Qubit 1 is fixed at 4.16 GHz. Qubit 2 and the coupler have Z lines. Each qubit couples to the coupler with \(\frac{g_{1c}}{2\pi}=\frac{g_{2c}}{2\pi}\approx72\) MHz, much more than the direct coupling. Qubit 2 moves to the gate frequency. Then a Z pulse on the coupler starts the coupling. CZ: 60 ns, 99.76 %. iSWAP: 30 ns, 99.87 %.

F. Yan et al., Phys. Rev. Applied 10, 054062 (2018) · Y. Sung et al., Phys. Rev. X 11, 021058 (2021), Fig. 1.

A ZZ-free iSWAP

During the iSWAP, the two qubits have the same frequency. Labels: \(|\text{qubit 1},\text{coupler},\text{qubit 2}\rangle\).

\(|200\rangle\) and \(|002\rangle\) are below \(|101\rangle\). Level repulsion moves \(|101\rangle\) up: \(\zeta>0\). This is the small conditional phase that comes with an iSWAP.

The second level of the coupler, \(|020\rangle\), is above \(|101\rangle\) and moves it down: \(\zeta<0\).

During the coupler pulse, \(\zeta\) changes sign (inset of c). For a 30 ns gate the total ZZ phase is zero: \(0.02\pm0.03^\circ\) per iSWAP (d). At 40 ns it is about \(2^\circ\) per gate.
